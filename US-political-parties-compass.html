How to use this — and how not to lose your work

1. Click around. Click any colored shape — a shaded region, a small dot inside it, a diamond, or an axis label — to see what it means. Click it again to close it.

2. Write notes. When something is open, there's a notes box under the description. Type whatever you want in there.

3. The most important part: your notes only exist on THIS screen, right now. They are not automatically saved anywhere. If you close the tab, close the Chromebook, or someone else uses this Chromebook next period, your notes will be gone — unless you save them first.

4. To save your notes: Type your name in the box at the top, then click "Save my version." A file will download — probably to your Downloads folder or Google Drive, depending on how your Chromebook is set up. That file is your notes. Keep track of it the same way you'd keep track of a saved Google Doc.

Don't double-click that saved file to open it. It's not a webpage — it's just data. Double-clicking it will show you a page of text that looks a little like this chart, but it's not the working chart, and nothing on it is clickable. That's a dead end, not your notes coming back.

5. To get your notes back next time: Open the original document again, not the saved file. Then click the "Load my version" button near the top of the page. A window will pop up asking you to pick a file — choose the one you saved last time. Then click on the same parties or dots you wrote notes on before, and your notes will show up again.

If you forget to save and lose your notes: it's okay, nothing bad happened, no work went missing anywhere. Just start over — the notes are the only thing that resets, everything else about the chart stays exactly the same.

Why "left" and "right" at all? It's not metaphorical — it's a seating chart. In France's National Assembly in 1789, supporters of the king and the traditional social order sat to the president's right; supporters of the revolution and equality sat to the left. Newspapers started describing votes that way, and the labels stuck for over two centuries. The economic axis on this chart is a direct descendant of that literal room.
